In December 2021, Governor Greg Abbot traveled to south Texas to inaugurate the first 880-foot stretch of the state's newly constructed wall on it's border with Mexico.

At the press conference, with cameras zoomed tightly on him against a backdrop of the 3-story high, slatted wall in Starr County, the Republican Governor declared the barrier to be impenetrable. He banged a mallet on a metal beam to drive home his point.

'Its heavy, and its wide', he said assuredly. 'People aren't making it through those steel bars'.

Three years and $3,100,000,000 later, Abbott may be right. Illegal immigrants and smugglers aren't breeching the bars. They don't have to because they can walk around them.

Today, that completed segment, now two miles wide, is an island of metal and concrete surrounded by farmland - hardly an obstacle for internally displaced people who have traveled sometimes thousands of miles to reach the US.

An investigation by 'The Texas Tribune' has identified for the first time where Texas has built its border wall, information the state keeps secret as it pours billions into the highly-touted infrastructure project. It has revealed that the unprecedented foray into what has historically been a federal responsibility - Texas is the first state to build its own border wall - has so far yielded little return on billions of dollars invested.

The 50 miles constructed through Nov., totaling 6 percent of the 805 miles the State has designated for building, are far from the endless barrier Abbott often presents the wall to be in video clips he shares on social media. The wall is not a singular structure, but is dozens of fragmented sections scattered across 6 counties, some no wider than a city block and others more than 70 miles apart. Each mile of construction costs between $17 million and $41 million per mile, depending on terrain, according to state engineers.
