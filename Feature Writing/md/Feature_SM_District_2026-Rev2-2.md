Leaguetown High School has 2,894 students in grades 9-12. English teacher Grace Baram's fourth-period class typically stays in her classroom to eat instead of going to the cafeteria for lunch since fourth period is split in half with lunch in the middle. One day in the fall, one student gave senior Gaby Laird a can of sardines from his lunch. Gaby had never tasted sardines, so she decided to film herself tasting the sardine and posted it on her Instagram account. Her video had 500,000 views and 80,000 likes by the end of the week.

The next week, another student brought a small brick of blue cheese for Gaby to try. After posting another video, which received a lot of attention, the class decided to work together to make lunch videos three times a week. Students brought supplies, including a ring light, microphones and tripods. They purchased editing software. After months of creating videos, the group has garnered more than a million followers and has started to earn money from the videos they create. They also started an Influencer Club, which meets after school on Thursdays, in addition to their regular lunch every day during fourth period.

On Thursday, March 26, they will host a guest speaker from BeGood Digital Marketing who will teach students about branding and how to maximize the money earned from social media partnerships. More than 150 students signed up to attend. You are writing for the issue of the Press to be distributed March 24.

## District Meet · 2026 Feature Writing Contest Tips and Sample Story

Contest Director: Give one copy to each judge to use during critiquing/judging. Also, staple one copy of the contest and one copy of the contest tips to each student's returned entry. The purpose of the tips is to provide immediate feedback to students. However, it is not meant to replace written comments from the judge.

1. Figure out what the story is about and its theme or focus. This story is about a group of students who started an Influencer Club after going viral on social media.
2. The lead of a feature story must grab the reader's attention. It does not have to be a certain length or include the 5Ws and H. It should set a scene and get the reader into the story. Although you have freedom to describe the scene as you think it might have existed, try to stay close to the prompt. Judges will not want you to wander off the subject too much.

When senior Gaby Laird popped that first sardine into her mouth, she didn't expect it to change her life. But she filmed her reaction and the video went viral on social media. Now, English teacher Grace Baram's fourth-period class has started its own Influencer Club.

3. Once you get past the lead, make sure you include a nut graph. It tells the reader what the story will be about. Follow the nut graph with a direct quote and then go into the transition/quote formula. In this story, the news peg is in the lead, but additional information is required.

## nut graph

An industry expert from BeGood Digital Marketing will speak to the club and more than 150 other students about careers in social media after school Thursday in the auditorium.

## direct quote

'Call it going viral,' Gaby said. 'Call it getting famous. Call it whatever you want. I call it me having fun with my friends in my English class.'

4. After the nut graph, the rest of the story can tell itself through the use of transition/ quote formula. Be sure you attribute after the quote or at least after the first sentence. An attribution should be: subject of attribution followed by the verb of attribution ('Olsen kind of transition between them. Also, make sure transitions provide information to help the action of the story and that the quote does not repeat information in the transition.

## transition/story-telling

Having lunch in Baram's classroom, the group created its first video when Gaby asked each person what they were having for lunch. Senior Paul Rhoades pulled out a can of sardines.

## direct quote

'I had never heard of anyone bringing sardines for lunch,' Gaby said. 'Paul said he tried them in Spain and they were actually really good. I just thought it was funny that he had this can of fish, so I opened Instagram.'

5. Always use third person. Don't say 'your students' or 'our students.' Also, do not editorialize. For instance, you should not say 'Go Gaby!' These statements are personal opinions and should not be used in a feature story.
6. Always use the verb 'said' as the verb of attribution. Don't say 'says,' 'stated,' 'feels' or any other synonym for 'said.'
7. Finally, look for a strong quote to use as a closing statement or a statement that will bring the reader back to the beginning of the story. It should give the reader a sense of satisfaction or resolution.

Paul said he is looking forward to hearing marketing expert Tim Sykes speak to students as they continue to grow their club and brand. In fact, Sykes has been following the students' work on their 'What's for Lunch?' account.

'These kids have some powerful instincts in terms of what audiences want to see and how to engage with different groups,' he said. 'There's just something so appealing about them.'

## SAMPLE STORY

When senior Gaby Laird popped that first sardine into her mouth, she didn't expect it to change her life. But she filmed her reaction and the video went viral on social media. Now, English teacher Grace Baram's fourth-period class has started its own Influencer Club. An industry expert from BeGood Digital Marketing will speak to the club and more than 150 other students about careers in social media after school Thursday in the auditorium. 'Call it going viral,' Gaby said. 'Call it getting famous. Call it whatever you want. I call it me having fun with my friends in my English class.' Having lunch in Baram's classroom, the group created its first video when Gaby asked each person what they were having for lunch. Senior Paul Rhoades pulled out a can of sardines. 'I had never heard of anyone bringing sardines for lunch,' Gaby said. 'Paul said he tried them in Spain and they were actually really good. I just thought it was funny that he had this can of fish, so I opened Instagram.' That first video of Gaby trying a sardine had 500,000 views and 80,000 likes by the end of the week. 'Paul opened the can and put it up to the camera so the audience could get a closeup of the fish,' she said. 'I smelled it and described it for the camera as almost a lemony, briny smell. Then I popped one in my mouth.' By that time, the whole class had gathered in the background of the video to share the reaction to the sardine tasting. 'People seemed to like it,' Gaby said. 'We laughed so hard when we were making that video. We all looked at each other and thought, 'We need to do this again.'' Gaby eventually renamed her account 'What's for Lunch?' as the group began bringing in unique foods to taste and taking turns being the taster with Gaby as the host. Since then, the group has earned more than a million followers and began making money for the videos they produce, which they share equally among the class. 'It's been a blast watching their social media project evolve the way it has,' Baram said. 'It's wild to see the this little joke they had take on a new life. This is an extra special group of seniors for me. I can't wait to see how they use their special powers in the future.' Paul, still a fan of sardines, has taken on the role of producer, managing equipment and helping manage the account. 'Every day when we're through with the lunch period, my side hurts from laughing so hard,' he said. 'I like that the videos are kind and encouraging. We're just eating foods you don't see every day and showing people what they're like.' Paul, who plans to do social media for his career, said he is looking forward to hearing marketing expert Tim Sykes speak to students as they continue to grow their club and brand. In fact, Sykes has been following the students' work on their 'What's for Lunch?' account. 'These kids have some powerful instincts in terms of what audiences want to see and how to engage with different groups,' he said. 'There's just something so appealing about them.'
