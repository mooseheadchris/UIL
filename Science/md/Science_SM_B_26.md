The decay products are pions, which are composed of up and down quarks, and (importantly) have zero strangeness. Thus, a quark flavor change must have taken place in order to go from a strange particle to decay products without strangeness. This suggests that the Weak force is involved. The lifetime confirms it. Strong force decays are very fast ( $\sim 10^{-23} s$ ) and even Electromagnetic force decays are reasonably fast ( $\sim 10^{-16} s$ ). Weak force decays are the only ones that are as slow as  $10^{-8} s$ . Thus, the Weak force is primarily responsible for the decay of the  $K^+$  meson.

- P19. (E) The important equation that relates the stretching of a spring to the force pulling on the spring is Hooke's Law:  $F = -k\Delta x$ . The sign indicates that the spring force is opposite to the direction of the stretching, but we don't need to consider directions to solve this problem, so we can ignore it. Noting that the force to stretch the spring is provided by the weight of each mass, we get: F = mg. Putting it all together (and removing the unneeded negative sign), we get:  $mg = k\Delta x = k(x x_0)$ , where  $x_0$  is the unstretched length of the spring. The plot is of length versus mass, or x versus m. Rearranging, we see:  $\frac{g}{k}m = x x_0 \rightarrow x = \frac{g}{k}m + x_0$ . Now we can tell that the slope of the line on the plot must equal  $\frac{g}{k}$ . To find the slope, we choose two points on the best fit line that are not data points. I shall choose (140g, 20cm) and (325g, 30cm). Converting to proper units gives us the points (0.140kg, 0.20m) and (0.325kg, 0.30m). Now we find the slope using these two points:  $slope = \frac{y_2 y_1}{x_2 x_1} = \frac{0.30 0.20}{0.325 0.140} = \frac{0.10}{0.185} = 0.54 \text{m/kg}$ . Equating this to the slope formula derived earlier, we obtain:  $0.54 = \frac{g}{k} \rightarrow k = \frac{9.80}{0.54} = 18.1 \approx 18 \text{N/m}$ .
- P20. (A) The electric field is proportional to the gradient (rate of change over distance) of the potential. So, the more steeply the potential changes, the greater the electric field. On the equipotential map, steep changes in potential appear as equipotential lines that are closely spaced together. The most closely spaced lines are clearly at point A, which is where the electric field magnitude is the greatest. Point B represents a steep turning point of one equipotential, but that is not indicative of a large electric field. Points C and E are local maximums in potential, but they are not near areas of large gradients. Maximum potential does not equate to maximum electric field. Finally, point D is where the lines are farthest apart, representing the area of lowest electric field magnitude.
